India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (the DPDP Act) sets rules for how organisations collect and use personal data. Universities and colleges are covered, because they hold information about students, parents, applicants, faculty and staff, often including children under 18.

This article is a plain-language overview to help leaders get started. It is general information, not legal advice, and the details should be confirmed with a qualified lawyer.

## Where things stand

The Act was passed in 2023. The government published the detailed Digital Personal Data Protection Rules in November 2025, and most of the obligations on organisations are being brought in in stages over about 18 months, with the main duties expected from around May 2027. Dates and details can change, so please check the current position before you plan around them.

The sensible reading is that institutions have a window to prepare, and that this window is best used.

## Key terms in simple words

- **Data principal:** the person the data is about, such as a student, parent or staff member.
- **Data fiduciary:** the organisation that decides why and how the data is used. In most cases this is the institution.
- **Data processor:** an outside company that handles data on the institution's behalf, such as a software or hosting provider.

## What the Act expects of an institution

In broad terms, institutions should be ready to:

- **Ask for clear consent** and explain, in plain language, what data is collected and why. Data should be used only for that purpose.
- **Collect only what is needed.** Information that serves no purpose should not be gathered.
- **Keep data accurate and secure,** with reasonable safeguards against misuse and loss.
- **Respond to requests,** such as a person asking to see, correct or delete their data, or to withdraw consent.
- **Report serious data breaches** to the authorities and to the people affected, in the manner the Rules require.
- **Delete data** when it is no longer needed for its purpose or required by law.
- **Handle children's data with extra care.** The Act expects verifiable parental consent for people under 18, and restricts tracking and targeted advertising aimed at children.
- **Provide a point of contact** who can answer questions about how data is used, and a way to raise grievances.

Penalties for serious failures can be significant, which is one more reason to prepare early.

## Why this matters on a campus

Education is full of personal data: admission forms, identity documents, marks, attendance, fee records, health information, photos and CCTV. It is spread across offices, spreadsheets, learning platforms and many outside vendors. Applicants and students are often minors or young adults who have little choice about handing information over.

## A practical starting plan

1. **Map your data.** List what personal data you hold, where it is stored, who can see it and which vendors process it.
2. **Review forms and notices.** Check that admission, enquiry and website forms explain clearly what is collected and why, and ask for consent properly.
3. **Review vendor contracts.** Make sure outside providers commit to protecting data and to telling you quickly about any incident.
4. **Set retention rules.** Decide how long each kind of record is kept, and delete what you no longer need.
5. **Prepare to answer requests.** Name who handles questions and corrections, and how fast they will reply.
6. **Plan for incidents.** Write a short response plan covering who acts, who is told and how.
7. **Train staff.** The most common failures come from everyday mistakes, such as sharing a spreadsheet by email or leaving records open.
8. **Name an owner.** Someone senior should be accountable for data protection, with support from IT, academic and legal teams.

## Connecting this to everyday systems

Good data protection is far easier when information is held in a small number of well-managed systems, not scattered across many files. Connected academic systems and strong cyber basics make most of the above simpler to achieve and to prove.
