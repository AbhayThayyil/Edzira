Every institution knows the pattern. A report is due for a regulator, an accreditation body or a ranking submission. Staff across departments are asked for numbers. Spreadsheets fly around by email, figures do not match, and a small team works late to reconcile them.

The effort is rarely about writing the report. It is about finding data that should have been easy to find. Here is why this happens and what to do about it.

## Why reporting is harder than it should be

In most institutions, academic information lives in many places:

- Admissions in one system or spreadsheet.
- Student records and results in another.
- Attendance kept by individual departments.
- Faculty and course details held by HR or the academic office.
- Feedback and placement figures held by yet another team.

Each source is maintained by a different group, with its own formats and its own definitions. Even a simple question, such as "how many students are enrolled this year?", can get different answers depending on who you ask and which date they use.

## The real problem: no single source of truth

When the same fact is entered in several places, the copies drift apart. A student changes programme; one record is updated and another is not. A report built from the wrong copy is wrong, and nobody finds out until someone checks.

A **single source of truth** means each piece of information is entered once, in one agreed place, and everything else reads from it. That is the foundation for fast, trustworthy reports.

## Five steps to get your data report-ready

### 1. Start with the reports you are asked for

List the reports your institution must produce each year, who asks for them and what data each needs. This tells you which information really matters, so you do not try to fix everything at once.

### 2. Agree on definitions

Decide, in writing, what each key term means. What counts as an "enrolled" student? On which date? How is a "graduate" counted? Disagreements about definitions cause more reporting errors than missing data does.

### 3. Name an owner for each data set

Every important data set needs a person who is responsible for its accuracy: admissions data, results, faculty records and so on. Without an owner, nobody fixes mistakes.

### 4. Reduce duplicate entry

Wherever staff type the same information into more than one place, look for a way to connect the systems or replace the spreadsheet. A student information system that covers the full student lifecycle removes a large share of duplicate work.

### 5. Build the reports once, then reuse them

Once the data is trustworthy, set up standard dashboards and report templates. The next accreditation or ranking cycle then starts from a working base instead of a blank page.

## What good looks like

Institutions that get this right tend to notice the same things:

- Leaders can see key figures, such as enrolment, results and attendance, without asking for a special report.
- Departments stop sending conflicting numbers.
- Preparing a submission takes days of checking rather than weeks of collecting.
- Students benefit too, because records are accurate and queries get answered faster.

## A note on starting small

You do not need to replace every system to improve things. A sensible first step is to pick one report that causes the most pain, trace where each number comes from, and fix that chain. The lessons carry over to the next report.
