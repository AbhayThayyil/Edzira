Most colleges do not struggle to buy digital learning tools. They struggle to know whether the tools are working. A platform is bought, a few enthusiastic faculty use it, and a year later nobody can say if learners are better off.

A short, well-defined pilot fixes this. It lets you learn on a small scale, with real students and real faculty, before you commit budget and effort to the whole institution. Here is a simple way to run one in 90 days.

## Before day one: choose a narrow scope

Pick **one department or programme** and **one or two courses**. A pilot that tries to cover everything produces results that are impossible to read.

Good pilot candidates have three things in common:

- A faculty lead who wants to take part, not one who was told to.
- A course with enough students to show a pattern, but small enough to support closely.
- A clear problem to solve, such as low attendance in a foundation course, slow assessment turnaround, or uneven content quality.

Write the problem down in one sentence. Everything else in the pilot should serve it.

## Days 1 to 15: set the goal and the measures

Agree on **two or three outcomes** and how you will measure each. Keep them simple and tied to the problem you chose. For example:

- **Engagement:** how many learners log in and complete activities each week.
- **Learning:** how results on a common assessment compare with the previous batch.
- **Experience:** what students and faculty say in a short survey at the start and end.

Record where you stand today. A pilot without a starting point can only produce opinions, not evidence.

At this stage also decide what "success" looks like, and what would make you stop or change course. Putting this in writing before the pilot begins protects you from judging the results too kindly afterwards.

## Days 16 to 30: prepare people and content

This is the stage that most pilots rush, and it decides how the rest goes.

- **Train the faculty involved.** A short hands-on session, followed by someone they can call when stuck, is worth far more than a long manual.
- **Prepare the first few weeks of content.** You do not need a whole semester built. Ready the first month and plan to improve as you learn.
- **Brief the students.** Tell them what is changing, why, and how to get help.
- **Check the basics.** Logins, devices, internet access and timetable slots should all work before the first class.

## Days 31 to 75: run it, and look at the data weekly

Start teaching. Then keep a steady rhythm:

- A short weekly check-in with the faculty lead: what worked, what did not, what needs fixing this week.
- A quick look at the engagement numbers each week. If a group of students has gone quiet, find out why early.
- One simple feedback moment around the midpoint, such as a five-question survey.

Fix small problems as they appear. A pilot is meant to be adjusted; changing the approach in week five is a sign it is working, not failing.

## Days 76 to 90: review honestly and decide

Compare the results with the starting point and the success measures you set at the beginning. Look at both the numbers and the comments.

Then answer four questions:

1. Did it solve the problem we chose?
2. What did faculty and students find hard?
3. What would it cost, in money and effort, to repeat this across more courses?
4. Should we **scale, adjust or stop**?

All three outcomes are useful. Stopping a tool that does not help is a good result, because it spares the institution a much larger mistake.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting too big.** More courses mean more variables and less learning.
- **Buying before testing.** Choose the platform because the pilot showed it fits, not before.
- **Skipping faculty support.** Technology rarely fails on its own; poorly supported people do.
- **Measuring nothing.** If the goal was never defined, every result can be called a success.

## Where to go from here

A pilot is a way to build confidence and evidence, not a final answer. If it goes well, the same plan can be repeated with the next department, with the lessons from the first one built in.
