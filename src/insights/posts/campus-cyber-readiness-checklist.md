Universities and colleges are attractive targets. They hold student records, research, payment details and personal information for thousands of people. Their networks are open by design, used by students and staff on many devices, from many places.

Cyber readiness does not require a large budget to begin. It starts with knowing where you stand. Use the ten questions below as a conversation starter for your leadership team, IT team and department heads. For each one, an honest answer of "we are not sure" is useful, because it shows where to look first.

## 1. Do we know what data we hold and where it lives?

You cannot protect what you have not listed. Make a simple inventory of the main systems and data sets: student records, results, fee payments, staff records, research data. Note who can access each one.

## 2. Who has access, and is it still needed?

Staff leave, students graduate, contractors finish. Check that accounts for people who have left are closed, and that access matches what each person's role needs today.

## 3. Is multi-factor authentication switched on for important accounts?

A password alone is easy to steal. A second step, such as a code on a phone, blocks most simple account takeovers. Start with email, administrator accounts and systems that hold student or financial data.

## 4. Are systems and software kept up to date?

Many incidents use weaknesses for which fixes already exist. Check that servers, laptops, network equipment and applications receive updates on a regular schedule, and that someone is responsible for it.

## 5. Are backups taken, protected and tested?

A backup that has never been restored may not work when you need it. Keep copies that cannot be changed from the main network, and test a restore at least once a year. This is also the best protection against ransomware.

## 6. Do students and staff know how to spot a scam?

Most incidents begin with a message that someone believes. Short, regular awareness sessions work better than a long annual one. Teach people to look for urgent requests, unfamiliar links and requests for passwords or payments, and to report anything suspicious without fear of blame.

## 7. Is the network sensibly separated?

Student Wi-Fi, administrative systems and any laboratory or research equipment should not all sit on the same open network. Separating them limits how far a problem can spread.

## 8. Do we know who to call when something goes wrong?

Write a short incident plan: who decides, who contacts whom, who talks to students and parents, and when to involve outside help. Keep a printed copy with phone numbers, because email may be down during an incident.

## 9. How do we manage vendors and cloud services?

Many campus systems are run by outside providers. Know which ones hold your data, what security they offer and what happens to your data if the relationship ends. Ask for these answers in writing.

## 10. Does leadership review this regularly?

Cyber readiness is not a one-time project. A short review each quarter, covering open risks, incidents and progress, keeps it a management matter, not only a technical one.

## How to use this list

Score each question simply: **yes**, **partly** or **no or not sure**. Then pick the **three** weakest areas and agree on one action and one owner for each, with a date. Small, finished improvements beat a long plan that never starts.
